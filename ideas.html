Markdown·open-architecture.md
01 · OPEN ARCHITECTURE

Build for choice. Design for change.

Open architecture provides choice and interoperability.

The problem

When data, business logic, and access are tightly coupled to individual tools, every new requirement can become another integration or migration project. The architecture starts limiting the business it was meant to serve.

My perspective

I am a proponent of Open Data Architecture: foundations built on open standards, interoperable interfaces, and components that can evolve independently. Openness should preserve practical choices about how data is stored, processed, governed, and consumed.

An open format alone is not enough. The real test is whether another engine, application, or team can use the data with its meaning and controls intact—and whether changing a component requires rebuilding everything around it.

The model

  • Portable foundations: accessible data formats, documented schemas, and clear ownership.
  • Interoperable interfaces: explicit contracts between producers, platforms, and consumers.
  • Independent evolution: separate storage, compute, and experiences where it creates meaningful flexibility.
  • Consistent governance: preserve access controls, lineage, and accountability across integrations.

When to use it

Evaluate a platform, plan a modernization, or introduce a new AI capability. Ask: What can we change without rewriting our data, definitions, and controls?

Illustrative example

A retailer wants forecasting, BI, and an AI assistant to use the same inventory data. Open interfaces and reusable data contracts let each consumer evolve while maintaining shared meaning and governed access.

02 · ENTERPRISE CONTEXT

Make the hidden operating system explicit.

Enterprise context connects data to the meaning and rules of the business.

The problem

Every enterprise already has a model of how it operates. It lives across applications, data, dashboards, policies, and people’s expertise. Much of what makes a business understandable is never captured in a table.

My perspective

I think of this distributed knowledge as the hidden operating system of the enterprise. AI creates a compelling reason to make it explicit: access to data does not automatically provide an understanding of the business.

An enterprise context layer connects entities, relationships, definitions, rules, and policies to the decisions they support. It needs owners and a way to evolve as the business changes.

The model

  • Business systems: where operational activity originates.
  • Enterprise reality: the entities, relationships, events, and data describing what happens.
  • Enterprise context: the meanings, constraints, policies, and exceptions that explain it.
  • Intelligence: reasoning and prediction grounded in that context.
  • Experiences: people, applications, and agents using the result and feeding back what changes.

When to use it

Scope an AI use case or investigate why technically correct answers miss the business question. Ask: What must this system understand, who owns that knowledge, and how will it stay current?

Illustrative example

An order record does not explain whether revenue can be recognized. An assistant also needs to understand fulfillment status, returns, credit holds, and the relevant business policy before it can give a useful answer.

Markdown·trusted-metrics.md
03 · TRUSTED METRICS

One number everywhere.

Trusted metrics turn shared definitions into consistent, testable outcomes.

The problem

Finance defines net revenue in a dashboard. An analyst rebuilds it in SQL. An AI assistant generates another interpretation. The discussion begins with reconciling numbers instead of deciding what to do.

My perspective

A shared definition is the starting point. Trust comes from proving that the definition is executed correctly across the tools that consume it—with the right grain, filters, time boundaries, and permissions.

“One number everywhere” means equivalent results for equivalent business questions, data snapshots, and authorized scopes. Different scopes may legitimately produce different numbers; those differences should be explainable.

The model

  • Define and own: agree on business meaning, calculation, dimensions, and accountable owners.
  • Execute consistently: preserve joins, grain, filters, and time semantics across consumers.
  • Authorize correctly: apply the intended access rules to each request.
  • Test and reconcile: compare expected outcomes across SQL, dashboards, applications, and AI.
  • Manage change: version definitions and check downstream impact before rollout.

When to use it

Evaluate a semantic layer, resolve conflicting KPIs, or enable conversational analytics. Ask: Can we demonstrate consistent business outcomes across consumers, including edge cases?

Illustrative example

Finance, Analytics, and an AI assistant answer the same net revenue question. Tests cover returns, discounts, duplicate joins, fiscal periods, and access scope before their results are compared.

Markdown·data-experience.md
04 · DATA EXPERIENCE

Design for the moment of use.

Data experience helps people, applications, and agents act on information.

The problem

A platform can be technically impressive while its users still struggle to find the right data, understand an answer, or take the next step. Infrastructure capability does not automatically translate into a useful experience.

My perspective

I view data experience as the point where architecture becomes valuable. It is the complete journey from a question to an informed action: discovering information, understanding its meaning, judging its reliability, and using it in a real workflow.

The consumer may be a person, an application, or an agent. Each needs appropriate context, access, and feedback—not necessarily the same interface.

The model

  • Discoverability: can consumers find the right information?
  • Understanding: are meaning, assumptions, and limitations clear?
  • Trust: can they inspect provenance, quality, and supporting evidence?
  • Timeliness: is the information fresh and fast enough for the decision?
  • Accessibility: can authorized consumers use it in their existing workflows?
  • Actionability: does it help them take a concrete next step?
  • Feedback: can they correct, challenge, and improve the experience?

When to use it

Design or assess a data product, dashboard, API, or AI assistant. Ask: Where does the consumer lose time, confidence, or the ability to act?

Illustrative example

A merchant asks why sales fell. A useful experience connects the change to inventory, promotions, and comparable periods; explains the evidence; and helps the merchant evaluate a response within the appropriate approval process.
